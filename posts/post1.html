A Beginner’s Introduction

R is a language and environment for statistical computing and graphics (R Core Team, 2026). At its heart, R is designed for people who work with data, whether that’s analyzing survey results, building predictive models, or creating publication-quality visualizations.

But R is more than just a programming language. The R Project describes it as an “environment”—a fully planned and coherent system for data manipulation, calculation, and graphical display. This environment includes:

  • Effective data handling and storage
  • Tools for calculations on arrays and matrices
  • A large collection of intermediate tools for data analysis
  • Graphical facilities for display on-screen or in print
  • A programming language with conditionals, loops, and user-defined functions

One of R’s greatest strengths is its extensibility. Users can add new functionality by defining functions or installing packages—collections of code, documentation, and data created by the community. Today, thousands of packages are available through CRAN (the Comprehensive R Archive Network), covering everything from machine learning to bioinformatics.

R is also free and open-source. It’s a GNU project, available under the GNU General Public License, which means anyone can download, use, and modify it without cost (GNU Project, 2026).

A Brief History of R

R’s story begins with another language: S. S was developed at Bell Laboratories in the 1970s and 1980s by John Chambers and colleagues as a tool for statistical computing (Chambers, 1998). It was designed by statisticians—not computer scientists—to link together calls to trusted FORTRAN packages.

R was created in 1993 by Ross Ihaka and Robert Gentleman at the University of Auckland, New Zealand (Ihaka & Gentleman, 1996). The name “R” is a playful nod to both its creators’ first names and its relationship to the S language.

The project was conceived in 1992, with an initial version released in 1995 and a stable beta version in 2000 (R Core Team, 2026). R became an official part of the GNU Project in December 1997 (GNU Project, 2026).

While R’s syntax resembles S, its underlying implementation is actually derived from Scheme, a dialect of Lisp (Ihaka & Gentleman, 1996). The developers first wrote an interpreter for a Scheme subset and then progressively modified it to resemble S.

Key milestones in R’s development include:

Year Milestone
1993 R is first created by Ihaka and Gentleman
1995 Initial version released
1997 R becomes an official GNU Project; CRAN launched
2000 Version 1.0 released—considered stable for production
2004 Version 2.0 introduced lazy loading
2013 Version 3.0 released

Today, R is developed by the R Development Core Team and supported by the R Foundation for Statistical Computing.

From RStudio to Posit

If you’ve been around R for a while, you’ve probably heard of RStudio—the popular integrated development environment (IDE) that made working with R much easier.

RStudio was founded by J.J. Allaire in 2009 as an open-source project, with Joe Cheng joining soon after to help develop the Shiny web framework in 2012. Over the years, the company brought on key contributors including Hadley Wickham (creator of ggplot2 and the tidyverse), Garrett Grolemund (creator of lubridate), and Yihui Xie (creator of knitr and R Markdown).

In 2020, RStudio became a Public Benefit Corporation (PBC) , codifying its open-source mission into its corporate charter and balancing the interests of community, customers, employees, and shareholders (Posit PBC, 2025).

In July 2022, the company announced a name change to Posit PBC. This wasn’t a move away from R—in fact, as Chief Scientist Hadley Wickham put it, “I’m not going to stop writing R code” (Wickham, 2022). Rather, the name change reflected the company’s broadening focus to support other programming languages like Python, making it a “single home for R & Python” data science.

Today, Posit continues to lead contributions to over 250 open-source projects, with the RStudio IDE remaining the premier environment for R development.

What is R Used For?

R is incredibly versatile. Here are some of its most common use cases:

Data Science & Machine Learning

R is a go-to language for predictive modeling, clustering, classification, and AI applications. Libraries like caret, randomForest, and xgboost make it easy to develop machine learning models.

Statistical Analysis & Research

R is extensively used in academia, scientific research, and economics for hypothesis testing, regression analysis, and survey data interpretation.

Data Visualization

Organizations use R to generate interactive dashboards, charts, and reports with packages like ggplot2, shiny, and plotly.

Healthcare & Bioinformatics

R plays a crucial role in genomics, drug discovery, and epidemiology through the Bioconductor package.

Finance & Quantitative Trading

R is used in financial modeling, risk analysis, and algorithmic trading with packages like quantmod and TTR.

Government & Policy Analysis

Government agencies use R for census data analysis, public health research, and social science modeling.

Resources for Beginners

Starting with R can feel daunting, but there are many excellent (and free!) resources to help you learn:

📖 Online Textbooks & Guides

  • R for Data Science (2nd edition) by Hadley Wickham and Garrett Grolemund — A free online book that introduces you to R, RStudio, and the tidyverse (Wickham & Grolemund, 2023).
  • YaRrr! The Pirate’s Guide to R — A clear, free online textbook for beginners (Phillips, 2025).
  • R-Tutor.com — A well-structured guide with examples (R-Tutor, 2026).

🎓 Free Online Courses

  • Software Carpentry: R for Reproducible Scientific Analysis — An introduction to R for people with no programming background (Software Carpentry, 2026).
  • RStudio’s Online Learning Guide — Extensive links to learning resources from getting started to interactive Shiny apps (Posit PBC, 2025).

🖥️ Learning Inside R

  • Swirl — An R package that teaches you R interactively directly in the R console. Just run install.packages("swirl") to get started (Swirl, 2026).

🛠️ Getting Started

  1. Download R from cran.r-project.org
  2. Download RStudio (now Posit) from posit.co
  3. Follow a beginner tutorial from the resources above
